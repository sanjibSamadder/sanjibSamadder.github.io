Lead & Income Predictor
Forecasting returns before money is spent.

A lead-buying business decided its monthly spend on each partner and offer largely by gut feel. I built a model that forecasts the leads, income and scrub cost a given spend should produce. I tested seven algorithms, documented three traps that would have made the results look better than they were, and deployed the winner as a live app.

01_problem.mdwhy this mattered

The business problem

The business buys leads from 77 partners across 40 offers, screens out ("scrubs") the poor-quality ones, and sells the rest to buyers. Every month someone decides how much to spend on each partner-and-offer combination.

Those decisions were based on recent memory, not on a forecast. Nobody could say in advance: "if we spend $X with this partner on this offer next month, what will we get back?"

The question I set

Given a partner, an offer, a planned spend and the month, predict three things at once:

  • Leads: how many leads the spend will buy
  • Sum Income: what the business keeps after costs
  • Sum Scrub Cost: how much will be lost on leads that fail screening
Constraint: only use information that is known before the money is spent. This rule turned out to matter more than any choice of algorithm (see lesson 1).

What the exploration showed

  • Volume metrics move together strongly (r = 0.74 to 1.00). Spend and scrub cost correlate almost perfectly (r ≈ 1.00).
  • About 11% of rows are statistical outliers on every volume metric. These are real high-spend months, not errors, so I kept them.
  • Income depends on lead quality, which isn't in the data. I expected it to be the hardest target to predict, and it was.

Three traps I caught

Each of these would have produced a better-looking number that the business couldn't actually rely on. All three are kept in the notebook so a reviewer can see how I caught them.

Using a number you don't have yet

An early tree used Sum Scrub Cost as an input. It took 88% of the model's feature importance. But scrub cost is only known about a month after the leads arrive, so at the moment of the spending decision the model would be relying on a number nobody has.

The holdout score that lied

One XGBoost run scored 0.917 on the holdout set, the best of any model. Cross-validation on the same setup gave 0.766. The single split had simply been favourable. From then on I compared every model on CV and holdout scores side by side.

What the model found

  • Spend is the dominant driver of all three outcomes. How much you put in explains most of what comes out.
  • One partner (Partner_59) stood out: it had a strong, consistent effect on lead volume across several independent models, so it's a real pattern, not a quirk of one model.
  • Income is the weak spot: every model struggled with it, which points to a missing quality signal rather than a modelling problem.

Where not to trust it

  • Partner and offer pairs with very little history.
  • Spend levels far outside anything seen in 2022–2026. Tree models don't extrapolate beyond the range they were trained on.
  • R² and MAE describe average performance, not certainty for any single forecast. The app supports human judgement; it doesn't replace it.
06_deploy.shstreamlit run app.py

From notebook to tool

The trained pipeline is saved together with the lists of known partners and offers, and served through a Streamlit app. A planner picks a partner and offer, enters a budget and month, and gets back expected leads, income and scrub cost. It's a forecast before spending, instead of a review after.

The app only offers partners and offers it was trained on, so it can't be asked to guess for an unknown combination.
